Arbitrarily Unconscious

“Rethinking: Meaning” · History of Modern Art現代藝術史

This is a critical writing on an imagined modern art curatorial.

List of Objects and Sketch of the Exhibition “Rethinking: Meaning”

  1. Jean Arp, According to the Laws of Chance. 1933. (Tate)
  2. André Masson, Battle of Fishes: 1926. (MoMA)
  3. Paul Cézanne, La Montagne Sainte-Victoire. c. 1900 (YUAG)
  4. Henri Matisse, Woman with a Hat (Femme au chapeau): 1905. (SFMOMA)
  5. Pablo Picasso, Ma Jolie: Paris, winter 1911–12 (MoMA).

“Rethinking: Meaning” features a circular exhibition space with five works spanning the first few decades of the twentieth century. The title itself points to some key questions: what is the meaning of art? What diverse forms could meaning take on?

The viewer is invited to consider these questions as they encounter Jean Arp’s According to the Laws of Chance. The curators were bold to display pieces of sugar paper on plain plyboard as the first piece of artwork. Instead of displaying a map or description of the exhibition on the very first wall, they chose to put a collage that conflicts with all intuitive understanding of the meaning of art. Any attempt at interpreting the irregular composition and random shapes of the black scraps would prove futile, and the thoughtful viewer would soon realize that the pieces of paper were surely not meticulously placed by Arp’s own hands after deliberate planning.

That is in fact correct — in 1916, Arp was not satisfied with his own creations, and he tore paper into pieces out of frustration and let them fall freely to the ground. Gravity thus revealed to him the “law of chance,” which he did not attribute to nature, but “providential guidance.”1 Either it was god or chance which composed the work, Arp played a minimal role in the creation of it. After he let go of the scraps, their positions should be chosen solely by gravity and air resistance.

However, upon a closer look, one would notice that none of the pieces of paper overlapped, something improbable just by chance alone. Arp would later explain that this work is indeed not made by pure chance, and that “accidents alone could not produce art; art required the imprint of human aspirations.”2 This complicates the question of who made this work: is it Arp, God, pure chance, or all of the above?

Regardless of how the viewer interprets it, one thing is certain: this work has no meaning. Dada artists like Arp were the pioneers who realized that art need not have meaning, and the rules set by bourgeois artists before them can be broken. As the first work of this exhibition, According to the Laws of Chance fills the minds of the viewers with the question: “what does art mean?” The viewer is thus encouraged to put behind all preconceptions about what art is and what art should look like, and let the works in the exhibition redefine their idea of meaning. The curators were intentional in eliciting this thought as viewers move to André Masson’s Battle of Fishes.

At first glance, one may think this work is a drawing made by a toddler: the messy sketches of fishes, the two large patches of sand, streams of blood-like red paint, and random curves of pencil and charcoal that occupy the remaining space of the painting. It would seem to the viewer that Masson did not put much conscious intention into this work. After serving in the French military during WWI, Masson was shocked at the brutality of war. He showed the inherent impetus of survival that he very personally felt through this battle of fishes.3

However, that impetus does not live in the conscious mind. Therefore, he chose to let his hand draw the fishes freely in the sea of unconsciousness, where objects only emerge ambiguous and uncertain. This is echoed by the fishes at the top right: the continuous lines connecting them obscure the boundaries between one fish and the next, as if objects in the subconscious are all ambiguous to some extent. The random traces of pencil thus seem to be what would later turn into fishes, yet they have not taken shape. Nothing in this painting is fully formed; each object only exists partially.

Upon closer inspection, the viewer would notice the uneven depth of the sand, and suddenly realize that Masson was also letting the “law of chance” play a part in this work: he smeared glue onto the painting and had sand fall from above, just like how Arp dropped pieces of paper on the plyboard. Here, the meaning is much clearer to the audience than Arp’s work, but it nevertheless is not a product of the conscious mind. Masson was one of the Surrealists who sought to find meaning in the unconscious, where creativity originates and the elements of what makes us human reside. Carrying on Dada’s mission to explore what the meaning of art could be, Masson kept the element of arbitrariness, but moved a step further to venture into the unconscious. There, he would find the desire for survival from the Great War nearly a decade prior. It is this desire which, at the time of this work, had receded from Masson’s consciousness into the unconscious, that materialized into the battling fishes.

After examining this work, the viewer is now equipped with the idea that meaning need not come from conscious, logical reason, and that the sea of unconscious is deep and rich in creativity.

To see Cézanne’s La Montagne Sainte-Victoire, the viewer must enter a small room, where the painting is placed at a distance from where the viewer sits. The audience would come into the room, observe the painting for a full minute, before the next batch of viewers take their turn. The curators were careful to obliterate any attempt at closely examining this work up close by making sure no viewer sits closer than five feet to the work. Moreover, an electrical circuit hidden behind the wall controls the glass covering the work, such that it slowly turns from transparent to translucent over the course of a minute, and only goes fully transparent in the very end. Only those who manage to stare at the painting for the full duration would be able to notice the gradual shift in visibility, as if the mountain is foregrounded by fog that clears little by little. I think this is the most clever way to see the painting, and perhaps even recreates how Cézanne saw the mountain.

Imagine a cozy morning in Aix-en-Provence, France, and Cézanne wakes up to see the limestone mountain outside the window. The mountain does not immediately reveal itself in full detail — possibly due to fog or, perhaps, because Cézanne’s consciousness has not fully registered it. He quickly picks up his pencil and brushes, and sketches the mountain as it becomes clearer and clearer, shading the darker parts with watercolor, as well as outlining the contours with graphite. As he works bit by bit on the painting, the mountain also reveals itself more and more. When Cézanne’s conscious mind finally gathered enough information from his eyes and formed a clear picture of the scene, he chose to put down his tools, rather than filling in the details. This wouldn’t make much sense to the average painter, until one realizes that the static image of the mountains matters much less to Cézanne than the process of its emergence. As Merleau-Ponty puts it, he captured “an object in the act of appearing, organizing itself before our eyes.”4

A viewer seeing La Montagne Sainte-Victoire would first encounter a blur of colors, from which no one could make out any sort of pattern. However, as the opacity of the glass decreases, the viewer’s eyes would find traces of contour, eventually grasping the outlines of the mountain as well as the patches of blue and grey that render it into a three-dimensional object. Through this process, one would need to puzzle out parts of the painting piece by piece, and therefore experience the process of emergence in full immersion.

This brings a new type of meaning to the audience: hidden beneath the static scene is the dynamic emergence that once took place, and the curators were clever to recreate that experience with modern technology.

So far, the works in this exhibition have been plain in appearance, from the black paper scraps on a white board, sketches of fishes and patches of sand, to the contours and shades of the limestone mountain in the process of its emergence. This is why, as viewers walk out of the room and immediately encounters Woman with a Hat by Henri Matisse, they would surely be shocked at the vibrance of the portrait of Matisse’s wife. The background is patched with a rainbow of colors, from light yellow, various shades of pink, purple-ish blue, to dark orange in the bottom left corner. Even the face of the woman is a mix of green, yellow, and pink, accompanied by her hair painted in reddish brown. Such an unnatural combination not only raises questions about what Matisse is trying to portray, but the person looking at it would also feel the painting through its colors.

When the viewers notice the patches of green and pink on the woman’s black dress and crutch, it would be apparent that Matisse was not trying to make an optically realistic depiction of what his wife looked like; rather, he was using the contrastive composition of colors to evoke a strong feeling in the audience. The viewer would then think back at Cézanne’s work and realize the connection between the two artists — they were both experimenting with new ways to record their sensation. While Cézanne does this by sketching the blurry scene before his consciousness makes it clear, Matisse is letting his feelings, at the moment of painting, control the composition. One should not, for instance, try to use any sort of color theory to interpret why he painted the shadows on the woman’s face as matcha green; the only reason was that he felt matcha green when he looked at his wife, and the same idea would apply to other parts of the painting as well.

With this Fauvist work, the curators showed that meaning can come from expression. In fact, Matisse said it himself in his Notes of a Painter: “composition is the art of arranging in a decorative manner the diverse elements at the painter's command to express his feelings.”5 The actual complexion of Matisse’s wife is of secondary matter — the feelings that her appearance brings to Matisse is what matters most, and Matisse’s clever use of color successfully delivered that sensation to viewers over a century later.

At the last stop of the exhibition, viewers would, at first glance, recall the feelings when they first entered the gallery and encountered Arp’s arbitrary work. Picasso’s Ma Jolie resonates with the work of the Dadaist through its seemingly random use of geometric shapes. Rectangles of different sizes occupy the majority of the painting, and larger triangles provide anchors to the scene.

As one looks closer, however, it would be clear that, unlike the Dada artists who try their best not to embed any meaning in their work, Picasso is encoding something between the abstract shapes. Contours of the feet in the center and the bottom right seem to imply that a person is involved, which is confirmed by the dress-like trapezoid to the right.

When viewers find that the six faint lines at the center allude to an instrument, the scene of a woman playing a guitar would gradually emerge, just like how the mountain made itself clear bit by bit as Cézanne saw it. By shifting away from the patterns we recognize in daily life, Picasso requires us to slowly decipher the relationship between the geometric shapes: although all the elements of the objects that we know of are present — the feet, strings of the guitar, the dress — they are not composed in the way we are familiar with. Those who identify this connection would realize that Picasso was able to let the subject appear gradually through the use of the arbitrary sign, rather than the signified one.6

When viewers turn their heads around to look at the first artwork, they will realize that Ma Jolie ties the start of the exhibition to its end. Arp’s According to the Laws of Chance showed that art can be arbitrary and meaning can be absent, yet Picasso’s work demonstrated that something that looks arbitrary can nevertheless contain meaning.

On its own, Ma Jolie also presents a new type of meaning, one that is not delivered through objects themselves, but through abstract representations of them.

The curators’ intent behind the design of the space is not hard to decipher: when visitors exit at the other end of the circular space and take another look at the Dada work, they will find the sequence of meanings they encountered throughout the exhibition forming a loop that invites them to think about consciousness, arbitrariness, and ultimately reconsider what the meaning of art is and what it could be.

However, I disagree that the history of modern art really forms a loop. For one, the works are not presented in chronological order in this exhibition. Although adjacent works do relate conceptually, one should not always assume a cause and an effect. Further, the Impressionists used contrasting, vibrant colors because the art before them could not express what they really felt; similarly, the Dadaists got rid of all meaning because they wanted to shatter the existing rules imposed on art. In this regard, art history is a progression where the successor expands upon, or completely overturns, its predecessor, and by no means is it a loop of the same concepts repeating over and over again.

In short, the curators chose to highlight the relationships between the works, at the expense of an accurate account of how art actually progressed throughout history. Nevertheless, “Rethinking: Meaning” demonstrates the tight connections between various movements of modernism, and every work in the exhibition would surely change what each viewer thinks about the meaning of art.
