The Contrasts and Patterns in The Tuileries Gardens, Paris

Formal analysis形式分析 · Édouard Vuillard, 1897–1900 · History of Modern Art現代藝術史

The Tuileries Gardens, Paris by Édouard Vuillard depicts a leisurely scene. Two women, one white and one black, are the first two figures that come to the viewer’s attention, both sitting behind a metal fence. A layer of trees lies behind them. Their leaves are dense and culminate in a blob of dark green that spans across the image. One can spot people under the trees: several to the left, one on the right, and perhaps some more blocked by the two women. The rest of the painting is occupied with two tall, similar buildings, as well as a column of black strokes and a cropped tree at the top right. I find that the artist created contrasts in social status and mood, and at the same time embedding repeating patterns in this complicated yet vibrant work.

Green is dominant in this painting, and the dark green of the leaves expand to other areas of the image — it gives the white trunk of the trees a tint of green, and the area below the trees is a monotone green as well. It even grows darker and darker as one looks downwards along the garments of the women on the left. There, the gold chair creates a stark contrast with the dark green on which it is superimposed, both in vibrance and in thickness of the strokes.

The lady on the right is dressed in plain black and white; I believe the difference in the color and quality of the dresses imply a hierarchical relationship between the two women, which also agrees with the social scene in late 19th century Paris. The other golden, foldable chair that no one is sitting on also contrasts the black woman’s clothing, while it matches the tone of the earth-yellow object superimposed on her.

The netted fence below the railing almost covers the bottom one-fifth of the painting, but the green cloth of the woman on the left seems to penetrate it as the fence disappears when it crosses her body before emerging on both sides. This counterintuitive depiction lets the garment display in full and further enhances the theme of the color green in the painting.

The black railings and fences guide the viewer’s attention horizontally, and create a sense of depth vertically through the change in detail and size of objects as the viewer moves from the two women to the trees in the back. The people in the back are dressed in various colors that contrast the green background; the red-and-white striped curtains of the building also stand out from the building’s white structure. Overall, the variety of colors creates a playful scene, where each part is trying to capture the viewer’s attention all at once.

As one focuses on the various human figures in this work, one may find many peculiarities. The two women at the front, for instance, look towards the bottom right of the painting, which suggests that they are not aware of the painter’s existence. However, what the women are looking at is ambiguous. The white woman’s gaze line up with the empty chair, but it could also lead to something below the platform which they are on. To understand what the woman may be looking at, the viewer must imagine what the platform is. If it is a bridge, the woman could be occupied by something happening on the river below them. If it’s on land, her eyes are most likely on the golden chair, perhaps thinking about someone she wished could sit on that chair with her on this leisurely day. However, her ambiguous gaze could also imply that she is simply taking a nap.

The woman on the right also lacks detail on her face, but she seems to look towards the yellow-and-white blob. I could not identify whether that is a piece of cloth wrapping around an infant, a light-brown hat with white decorations, or some other object. Regardless of the interpretation, however, it is clear that the two women are not in a good mood. This resonates with the paintings on bourgeois women in that period, which often depicts them as bored or melancholic. The woman to the right, who is likely the servant of the other woman, is bored but must sit alongside her as she ponders.

Turning our attention to the people at the center left of the painting, we see that none of them are painted in detail nor as a full person; some have their head cropped by the tree, while others only reveal a portion of their upper body. This connects them to the working class, whose bodies are often not painted in full, in contrast to the bourgeoise. In this context, while the wealthy individuals can enjoy their time far from the buildings and trees, the working class could only spend their time by strolling in the woods. But they nevertheless enjoy their time, and they might even be happier than the bourgeoise.

One can also spot strokes of red and black towards the center right of the painting. The red figure is likely a person, whose torso is again cropped by the woman’s head. The black object is likely a bird, since another bird with a bright, red eye is present at the center, sitting atop the green hat. Although we could not make out the emotions of the people under the trees, their relative positions imply that most of them are walking as a group, which gives the ambience of a relaxing, joyful afternoon family stroll.

When one looks closer, one may find patches of color that doesn’t make intuitive sense, both with regards to their position and their color. A nearly-square patch of gold is situated immediately to the left of the white women’s body. It creates a balance with the thin, golden chair which the women is sitting on, but it is difficult to come up with an explanation for why it is present.

Behind the second layer of the netted fence, one may find near the middle a stroke of light pink, a color not seen anywhere else in this work. Additionally, the surface which the women reside on shift from white to brown as we move our focus rightwards, deepening the question of where exactly they are.

Between the two tall trees that extend towards the top edge, patches of white from the buildings also burst out, breaking the dominant blob of dark green leaves. On a larger scale, the black-and-white rectangle at the top right leaves the viewer with an open question of what it represents. One may interpret it as the side of the buildings, but that would render the rest of the painting unphysical, since none of the other parts of the work show a change in depth as we move rightwards. It could also be the trunk of a very tall tree, but that would lead to more questions regarding its presence. Nevertheless, these intricate uses of color all add a layer of liveliness and mysteriousness to the painting.

As we again observe the work as a whole, we may realize that there are many repeating patterns throughout the painting, as well as a gradient from order to disorder. The buildings are made up of identical elements, and their only difference is the placement of the curtains. They are the elements with the most order, which could link to the abundance of mechanic productions as France advances further and further into industrialization.

Moving away from these tall, man-made structures, the trees also show a distinct pattern. In particular, these elements of nature seem to appear in three — the three yellowish-green, elliptical blurbs hover rhythmically above the dense, dark-green leaves. The viewer may only see the two tall trees at first glance, but a tree with a similar height is also present, albeit cropped by the right edge, making both the yellow blobs and the large trees show up in trios.

The two women also partition the space under the trees into three sections with nearly equal sizes, with the center piece empty and the other two, which contain people and birds, more lively. This suggests a way to view the bottom half of the image as three vertical sections, while the painting as a whole is also sliced into three parts — one time by the green leaves extending horizontally, and once more by the railings. Amid the complicated scene created by the colors, these geometries provide anchors for the viewer’s attention. They guide one’s eyes horizontally and vertically through the rhythmic patterns, and the viewer is likely to find new, intricate details through repeated sweeps along the image.

Édouard Vuillard used a wide palette of colors to demonstrate contrasts at many levels, from the layering of nature and man-made structures, to the size and mood of the people, to differences in social status. The work leaves many questions open to interpretations and adds mystery to a lively Paris garden scene.
